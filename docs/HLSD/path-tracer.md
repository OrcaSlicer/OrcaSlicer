# Path Tracer

## Purpose

The interactive GPU path tracer provides physically based progressive rendering in both the 3D model view (Prepare) and the sliced toolpath preview (Preview). It visualizes material optics, realistic lighting, soft contact shadows, surface gloss, and volumetric transmission through translucent 3D printing filaments. When enabled in application preferences (`SETTING_OPENGL_PATHTRACE`), the path tracer renders continuously in the background during camera stillness and converges progressively across frames, providing accurate depth and material perception without stalling interactive navigation.

Quality and performance are controlled by a single preference (`SETTING_OPENGL_PATHTRACE_QUALITY`) with three presets:
- **Low:** 64 max samples, 1 indirect bounce, 4 max transmission bounces, 4 shadow hits, 8.0 ms tile budget, 15–50% dynamic orbit resolution, no subsurface scattering.
- **Medium (Default):** 128 max samples, 2 indirect bounces (1 in Preview, where bounce rays start inside dense toolpaths, run incoherent and cost several times a camera ray, while the second bounce adds about half a percent of brightness), 8 max transmission bounces, 8 shadow hits, 10.0 ms tile budget, 18–65% dynamic orbit resolution, lateral diffusion and thin-wall backlight.
- **High:** 256 max samples, 2 indirect bounces, 12 max transmission bounces, 8 shadow hits, 12.0 ms tile budget, 20–75% dynamic orbit resolution, lateral diffusion and thin-wall backlight.

## Acceleration structure (TLAS / BLAS)

Scene geometry is organized in a two-level bounding volume hierarchy consisting of a top-level acceleration structure (TLAS) over instance bounds and bottom-level acceleration structures (BLAS) for individual meshes or toolpath groups. Modifiers and unprintable parts are no part of the print: they stay out of the scene, and the rasterizer draws them over the path traced image as in the plain view.

- **BLAS (Bottom-Level BVH):** Built asynchronously using a Surface Area Heuristic (SAH) with binned splitting on a background worker thread (`_build_pathtrace_scene_async`). Node data is packed into 32-byte GPU nodes (`PathTraceGpuNode`) flattened in breadth-first order so that the two children of each inner node occupy contiguous array indices (`[child, child + 1]`). BLAS building accepts an atomic cancellation token (`cancel_token`) to abort promptly when the camera or scene geometry changes.
- **TLAS (Top-Level BVH):** Constructed on the main thread over instance world-space axis-aligned bounding boxes (AABBs). In scenes with four or fewer instances, rays test instances directly with minimal overhead; in scenes with more than four instances, a binary top-down bounding volume hierarchy (`TlasBuffer`, binding 4) is traversed logarithmically with a fixed-size traversal stack and distance-ordered child visiting.
- **Identity Transform Fast-Path:** Instances whose transform is an identity matrix (marked via instance metadata) bypass local ray transformation and normal transformation, reducing register pressure and memory bandwidth in both primary and shadow traversal.
- **Deferred Normal Reconstruction:** Triangle intersection records only the closest hit primitive index without evaluating edge cross-products or vertex transformations. Geometry normals and edge-length logic are reconstructed once per ray after traversal completes.
- **Shading Normals:** Meshes in Prepare carry vertex normals per triangle (`NormalBuffer`, binding 5), each octahedron encoded in 32 bits. At a corner they average the faces around the vertex that bend from the corner's face by less than 30 degrees, so curved surfaces shade round while the edges of a part stay sharp. They follow the Smooth normals preference, as the rasterized views do: without it every triangle shades flat. Rays leaving the front of a smooth triangle start from the point lifted onto the surface its vertex normals describe (Hanika, "Hacking the Shadow Terminator"), so that shadow terminators follow that surface instead of the facets. Toolpaths in Preview have no vertex normals: their beads have their own analytical profile.
- **Buffer Layout:** Geometry is uploaded to OpenGL Shader Storage Buffer Objects (SSBOs):
  - Binding 0: Node buffer (`NodeBuffer`)
  - Binding 1: Triangle buffer (`TriangleBuffer`)
  - Binding 2: Instance buffer (`InstanceBuffer`) with local-to-world and world-to-local transformations
  - Binding 3: Shading metadata (`ShadingBuffer`) containing linear albedos, layer heights, and extrusion roles
  - Binding 4: Top-level acceleration structure (`TlasBuffer`)
  - Binding 5: Vertex normals of smooth triangles (`NormalBuffer`), Prepare only

Transformations of objects or plates update only the instance buffer without triggering a BLAS rebuild. Color changes in Prepare or Preview update vertex shading buffers in-place via `glBufferSubData` without rebuilding either hierarchy, triggering triangle buffer uploads only when opacity values change.

## Frame scheduling and interactivity

To preserve high UI responsiveness (≥ 60 FPS) during scene interaction and convergence:
- **Interactive Pass:** While the camera or the scene changes, and as the first frame after any reset, a single reduced-resolution pass (`is_interactive_pass = true`) runs the same path tracer as the progressive passes, so the image keeps its look while orbiting: one sample through pixel centers, the key light from the center of its disk, one indirect bounce, a lower cap on indirect contributions, and the denoiser at full strength.
- **Persistent Images:** All images are allocated at the full render size (at most 1440 pixels on the long side) and only follow the canvas size. The interactive pass renders into their top left corner and has its own display images, sampled with sub-UVs in the composite; it stays on screen until the first full-resolution sample is complete, so releasing the mouse never reallocates textures or stalls on a full frame in one dispatch.
- **Interleaved Tile Scheduler:** From sample 1 onward, progressive accumulation dispatches fixed 128×128 tiles within a dynamic GPU frame budget (8–12 ms), which leaves the rest of a 60 Hz frame to the rasterizer and the UI. Tiles are dispatched in a quasi-random Golden-Ratio order ($(\text{cursor} \cdot \text{stride}) \pmod{\text{total\_tiles}}$) that distributes compute load evenly across sky and dense object geometry, preventing latency spikes. The display is only resolved when a sample completes, so it never shows tiles at different sample counts.
- **Dynamic Orbit Scaling:** During active camera rotation or dragging, the interactive resolution follows an 8 ms GPU target (`m_pathtrace_dynamic_orbit_scale`). The cost of a frame follows its pixel count, so the scale meeting the target follows from the scale and time of the measured frame: the controller drops straight to it and grows back gradually.
- **Interactive Reprojection:** Each interactive frame projects the surface point of every pixel into the previous interactive frame and, where that pixel saw the same surface (same distance within 2%, similar normal) or the background there too, blends its sample with the history kept there, over at most 6 frames: few enough for the shading to follow the camera bound key light. The history is read from the nearest pixel, not interpolated, which would blur the image every frame and blend what lies behind translucent surfaces in neighboring pixels. It is a copy of the last interactive frame's accumulation, bed term and first surfaces, dropped when anything but the camera changes. Each frame takes a new jittered sample, and the denoiser filters with a narrower luminance edge stop once samples accumulate.
- **ImGui Decoupling & Scene Caching:** Active ImGui widget adjustments (layer slider, palette controls) suspend compute dispatches and blit the existing display image to maintain maximum UI framerates. Once `max_samples` is reached, `GLCanvas3D::_is_scene_cacheable()` activates `SceneCache`, reducing idle GPU usage to 0%.

## G-code toolpath meshing

In Preview, G-code extrusion toolpaths are extracted into triangle geometry tailored by extrusion role:
- Outer walls are diamonds in section, the shape the rasterized preview gives every bead, with mitered joints between adjacent segments of identical role on the same layer.
- **Analytical Layer Profile:** In Preview, `layer_profile_mode` rebuilds the shading normal of the bead facets seen by the camera from the height of the hit within its layer. A bead pressed onto the layer below is a stadium in section: its side is a half circle as high as the layer, whose normal turns from the side at mid height to straight up or down at the seams between layers. That turn puts a narrow highlight on every layer, and on a sloped surface the layers above hide the part of each bead facing away from the slope, so where the hits land follows the slope. The profile never fades with distance: the jittered samples of a pixel land at different heights of the beads, and accumulating them averages the profile over the pixel, the sheen of a print seen from afar. The flat ribbons of top and bottom surfaces, solid infill and bridges are beads seen from above and take the profile the roofs of the perimeters around them show: across each line the normal turns from straight up on its crest to the side at its edges, which leaves a groove between neighboring lines, so the lines of a top surface stay visible. Their free ends and sharp turns are rounded half disks. Ironing is the exception: its lines overlap each other many times to smooth the surface, so its ribbons shade flat and lie a hair above the top surface they iron. Slanted deck facets of perimeters are bead sides. Each quad of the toolpath mesh is two triangles with a fixed corner order, and the second carries 0.25 on its facet kind: from it and the barycentric coordinates the shader knows where the hit lies along and across the bead, whatever order the BVH puts the triangles in.
- Inner perimeters, infill, and solid decks emit compact upper bevels or planar ribbons to keep traversal depth low. Ribbons lie at the top of their layer, level with the crests of the walls around them.
- Seam markers are generated as diamond octahedra exempt from shadow casting and subsurface diffusion.
- All facets, end caps included, wind outward, which the facing of a hit relies on.
- Height and move clipping (`z_min`, `z_max`, `move_min`, `move_max`) cull primitives both during ray-box intersection tests and inside leaf intersection routines.
- The layers around the ones the sliders show are greyed or dimmed as in the rasterized preview: libvgcode's `LayerShading` gives the rule its toolpath shaders apply, and the path tracer applies it to the toolpaths the camera sees, by the top of their layer, which the triangles carry. Greying replaces the albedo with the rasterizer's grey. Dimming is a view aid, not a material: it darkens the whole surface, highlights included, by the linear equivalent of the rasterizer's 8 bit sRGB scale, since on dark filaments the highlights would otherwise make most of the brightness and hide it. Light reaching other parts from a dimmed layer is not dimmed.

## Lighting

The light rig matches the rasterizer's shading model, so that a path traced filament has the color the rasterizer gives it: a white Lambertian surface facing the key light takes 0.70 of irradiance (divided by π) from it, 0.22 from the fill light, and 0.19 + 0.05·n.z from the sky.

- **Key light:** a disk of 0.045 rad, sampled only by next event estimation. BSDF-sampled rays never hit it, so each light has exactly one estimator and needs no multiple importance sampling. It follows the camera unless static shadows are selected.
- **Fill light:** directional and unshadowed, evaluated through the BSDF at every surface. It stands for a large soft box, so its highlight is never sharper than a GGX alpha of 0.35.
- **Sky:** a dome whose radiance rises linearly with the height of the direction, $L = 0.19 + 0.075\,z$, so that its irradiance has the closed form above. Rays that escape see it; paths that end at their bounce limit, and the interactive pass, take its analytic irradiance instead of sampling it.
- **Bed:** rasterized, so the path tracer only sees it in reflections and indirect light, with a dark albedo like the default plates: a bright one would make objects mirror a bed the viewer cannot see. Seen from below, where the rasterizer draws the bed see-through, the path tracer leaves it out, so that the print shows through it.

## Materials

Every filament is a dielectric of index 1.5 (exact Fresnel) with a GGX microfacet interface, sampled from its visible normals. The roughness comes from the shading parameters; after the first bounce it is raised to a floor (path space regularization), which removes most caustic fireflies.

Filament colors are those the rasterized views show, through `adjust_color_for_rendering()`: near black lifted to a grey, the more the more translucent, and an opacity of at least 10%. libvgcode gets them adjusted for Preview, and the path tracer adjusts them itself in Prepare, so that a filament looks the same in both.

The Glossy toolpaths preference means in Preview what it means in the rasterized views. Off, translucent toolpaths are matte and opaque ones get a soft, broad highlight, their GGX alpha raised to 0.5, instead of the glossy plastic one.

- **Opaque filament:** a Lambertian base under the GGX coat, the diffuse part scaled by the Fresnel transmission in and out and normalized to reflect exactly the albedo at normal incidence. One direction is sampled from the mixture of the two lobes and weighted by the mixture pdf.
- **Subsurface scattering of opaque filament:** Burley's normalized diffusion, with a mean free path of 0.12, 0.35 or 0.8 mm by SSS mode (at most 0.35 mm for toolpaths) and per-channel diffusion lengths from the albedo. On camera-visible surfaces a radius is importance sampled from the profile of a random channel, projected on the tangent plane, and the surface found there decides the visibility of the key light; the cosine keeps the normal of the visible point, so layer lines and bead shapes stay crisp while shadow edges soften and take the color of the deeper channels. It is skipped when the diffusion length is below half a pixel. On the medium and high presets a surface lit from behind also takes the light diffusing through a thin wall of its own part.
- **Translucent filament:** the opacity of a filament color is the share of light 0.4 mm of it does not let straight through (see [translucent-filaments.md](translucent-filaments.md)). The pigment and haze diffuse that share in the filament color, as an opaque filament does: half back and half on through a clear wall, all of it back from an opaque one, so the look runs continuously from a clear tinted filament to the opaque one. The light going straight through takes the color of the dye, about four walls of it bringing white light to the hue of the color, so thick parts saturate.
  - Printed walls are thin slabs with near parallel faces, so light leaves them in the direction it came in: each crossing of a surface takes the Fresnel reflection of the interface and the absorption and scattering of a wall. A print is no solid block of plastic: it neither bends the view through it nor reflects totally inside, so the path tracer does not refract. In Preview only the first toolpath wall a path crosses reflects: behind it, beads lie fused to their neighbors, plastic against plastic, where nothing reflects, and their reflections of the sky would lay a haze over everything inside a translucent print.
  - In Preview the wall is half the bead's path length: a closed bead is crossed twice, a single ribbon once.
  - In Prepare a part is closed and stands for a print: each crossing passes one 0.4 mm wall, and the inside is sparse infill of which 1.5% is plastic. A ray knows it was inside when it next hits a back face, so no medium stack is kept.
- **Seam markers** are half lit and half self-illuminated, so they stay readable, and cast no shadow.

## Shadows

Shadow rays towards the key light take the transmittance of the translucent triangles they cross, in any order: the Fresnel transmission of each interface and the transmittance of the wall behind it, half a bead in Preview or one wall in Prepare, which is the dye-tinted light going straight through plus the diffused light leaving on the far side in the filament color. In Prepare they also take the Beer-Lambert attenuation of the infill, summed with the sign of each triangle's facing so that the terms of a closed part leave the length inside it. An opaque triangle ends the ray, as does the walls' transmittance falling below 1%; past the shadow hit budget of the preset the partial transmittance is kept.

## Sampling and robustness

- **Sample sequence:** each pixel draws hash-based Owen-scrambled Sobol points (Burley 2020). Every 2D dimension pair shuffles the sample index with its own seed, which decorrelates pairs across bounces and pixels while keeping each pair stratified over the samples of a pixel.
- **Pixel filter:** the camera ray of a sample is offset from the pixel center by a Gaussian of 0.45 pixels (filter importance sampling), so the accumulation filters the image with that Gaussian rather than a box over the pixel. A box lets through the frequencies around the pixel pitch, and the layer lines of a print seen from afar fold back from there into moire rings however many samples accumulate; the Gaussian damps them for a barely softer image.
- **Ray origins** are offset off the surface by a few ulps along the geometric normal, on the side the ray leaves (Wächter and Binder), and hit points are rebuilt from barycentrics, so the offset holds at any coordinate magnitude and ray length. A direction below the geometric surface ends the path.
- **Kernel structure:** GLSL inlines every call, so each call site of a traversal adds a full copy of it to the kernel, and the register pressure of those copies slows every ray, taken or not. The path loop therefore traces one ray per iteration from a single call site, and first the one shadow ray the previous vertex queued; a vertex that needs a subsurface probe ray parks its continuation until the probe is answered. Small scenes and the top level BVH share one call site of the bottom level traversal, whose stack holds node indices only. Shadow rays visit the nearer child first too: in dense toolpaths the occluder is mostly a neighbor bead, which that finds at once. Subsurface probes only traverse their own part.
- **Shadow rays through translucent walls** cannot stop at the first hit and must cross every wall up to the light, through every box of the BVH on their way: in translucent toolpaths they make most of the cost. They go through a Russian roulette: those of highlights on walls behind the first wall a path crosses, dimmed by the walls in front, are traced a quarter of the time, and those of bounced light half the time, at the inverse weight. The weight applies after the firefly cap, which would otherwise cut the scaled contributions more often and darken the image.
- **Variance control:** Russian roulette on the throughput after each bounce, a luminance cap on indirect contributions, and a sample cap before accumulation; samples with NaN or infinite values are dropped.

## Denoising and compositing

The path tracer accumulates, per pixel, the radiance of everything but the rasterized bed and background with the coverage of the path, and the bed term: the ratio of the light reaching the bed to that of an unoccluded bed, times the transmittance of the translucent parts in front of it. It also keeps the running average of the first surface's normal, distance, albedo and class (background, bed, opaque, translucent).

`pathtrace_denoise.comp` resolves the accumulation into the display images whenever a sample completes. Three a-trous passes (steps 1, 2, 4; a step of 8 leaves blocks around sharp highlights) filter the radiance divided by the albedo of opaque surfaces, with edge stops on normal, distance, albedo, class and luminance, and filter the bed term the same way. The luminance edge stop of the radiance follows the variance of each pixel's average, as in SVGF: the path tracer keeps the sum of the squared luminance of the samples (`moment_image`), the first pass takes the variance of a 3x3 neighborhood, and each pass carries it on. Where a pixel is still noisy, neighbors of a different luminance blend in; where it has converged, its edges and texture stay, so the filter limits itself and is not blended out. On a test scene this halves the error to a 1024 sample reference at 16 and 64 samples, where a filter fading with the sample count blurred converged edges. The bed term keeps no variance: its filter is blended over the unfiltered one with a weight of $\min(1, 4/\sqrt{n})$ for $n$ samples. Interactive frames do not keep the variance either; they are filtered at full strength, with a wide luminance edge stop for a single sample.

The tone curve is the identity up to 0.8, so lit filament colors stay exact; above it, it compresses highlights on the largest channel and scales the color by the same factor, which keeps the hue of bright saturated filaments, and light far above white runs towards white, as on film. Filmic curves such as AgX also bend the colors below white and bleach every lit surface, which a slicer showing filament colors cannot afford. The composite first multiplies the rasterized bed by the bed term (stored halved in RGBA8 and blended with `GL_DST_COLOR, GL_SRC_COLOR`), then draws the path traced image over it, preserving bed markings inside colored and ambient shadows.
